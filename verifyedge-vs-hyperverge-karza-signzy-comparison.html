VerifyEdge vs HyperVerge vs Karza vs Signzy: An Honest Comparison of Identity Verification APIs for Indian Banks

Published on 5 September 2026 • by SecureEdge Team

If you're a bank, NBFC, or fintech evaluating identity verification API vendors in India right now, you have several credible options in front of you, and that's actually the honest starting point for this post - not a dramatic claim that one vendor obviously beats the rest on every count. VerifyEdge, HyperVerge, Karza (now part of Perfios), and Signzy are all real, established players serving regulated financial institutions, each with a different history, a different specialization, and a different way of packaging what they sell. A procurement team comparing them deserves a comparison that says what actually differs, not a sales page dressed up as an article.

That's what this post tries to do. We're not going to invent statistics about accuracy percentages, client counts, or uptime numbers for competitors we don't have verified access to - if we don't know something specific and can confirm it, we'll say so plainly and point you toward verifying it directly with the vendor. What we can do is lay out what's publicly known about how each platform positions itself, walk through the dimensions that actually matter in a real evaluation, and be upfront about where VerifyEdge fits and where it doesn't necessarily win outright.

The Three Vendors You'll Likely Run Into

Any bank or NBFC scoping out verification API vendors in India will almost certainly come across these three names alongside VerifyEdge. Here's what each is publicly known for, based on how they position themselves.

HyperVerge

HyperVerge has built a strong reputation specifically around biometric verification, liveness detection, and deepfake defense. They publish ISO 30107 passive-liveness certification claims and offer a dedicated product, DeepfakeSafe, aimed at the growing problem of synthetic and injected biometric fraud. Their client base spans banks, NBFCs, insurance companies, and capital markets firms, among other sectors. If a bank's primary evaluation criterion is depth of biometric and liveness technology specifically, HyperVerge is a name worth including in that conversation.

Karza Technologies (Perfios)

Karza operates today as part of Perfios, which describes itself as an "AI Operating System" serving banks, NBFCs, fintechs, and insurers. The combined offering covers KYC, video-KYC with liveness, and financial document verification - reading bank statements, ITR filings, GST data, and payslips - alongside a dedicated background-verification product and a deepfake-detection capability. Perfios positions itself toward large enterprise clients, and the breadth of financial-document analysis in particular is a notable strength if that's a significant part of your underwriting stack.

Signzy

Signzy positions itself as a broad platform spanning KYC, KYB and UBO (ultimate beneficial owner) checks, AML/PEP/sanctions screening, background screening, and bank account verification, all within one platform aimed at large enterprises. The breadth is the pitch here - one vendor relationship covering identity, business, and compliance screening together, rather than assembling those from separate point solutions.

Worth noting: none of these three publish public, self-serve pricing on their websites. Evaluating any of them in detail typically means starting a sales conversation and requesting a custom quote, which is a common and unremarkable pattern in enterprise software generally - it isn't a red flag on its own, just a fact about how you'll need to budget your evaluation timeline.

Why Pricing Transparency Matters for Your Procurement Process

This deserves its own section because it's easy to underrate how much friction custom-quote pricing adds to an internal approval process, separate from whatever the eventual number turns out to be. A bank or NBFC's procurement cycle usually needs some kind of indicative cost estimate before anyone will approve time for a full vendor evaluation, let alone a pilot - budget owners want a rough sense of per-call or per-verification cost before committing a product manager's time to sales calls with four different vendors.

When pricing is published - even as indicative, volume-tiered numbers rather than your exact final rate - a product or risk team can build a cost model against expected verification volumes in an afternoon and bring a real estimate into an internal budget conversation. When pricing requires a custom quote, that same estimate needs a sales call (sometimes several) before you have a number to work with at all, which stretches out an evaluation that's already competing against four other line items for the same team's attention this quarter.

To be clear, custom-quote pricing isn't a sign of anything wrong with a vendor - it's an extremely common model in enterprise software, and it can reflect real complexity in how a large platform prices out different products and volume tiers. But if your institution values being able to build a cost estimate without a sales conversation first, that's a legitimate and practical factor to weigh, not just a nice-to-have.

Questions to Ask Any Verification API Vendor

Regardless of which vendor you're evaluating - VerifyEdge included - these are the questions worth asking before you sign anything. A vendor's willingness to answer them clearly and specifically, rather than with a generic marketing line, tells you almost as much as the answers themselves.

  • What's the request-level SLA, not just a headline uptime number? A "99.9% uptime" claim on a website says little about what happens to your specific verification call during a slow response or a source-registry outage. Ask for the actual SLA document - response time guarantees, what counts as a breach, and what remedy you get if it's breached.

  • Does the platform detect injection attacks, not just presentation attacks? Presentation-attack detection (catching a photo held up to a camera, say) is table stakes now. Injection attacks - where fraudulent biometric data is fed directly into the verification pipeline rather than presented to a camera at all - are a meaningfully different threat, and not every vendor's public materials make clear whether their detection covers both.

  • Where is verification data stored and processed? For a regulated Indian financial institution, data residency isn't optional - ask specifically where servers are located, whether data is stored in India, and how that maps to RBI and DPDP Act expectations for your institution.

  • How long does integration actually take, and is sandbox access self-serve? A vendor that gates sandbox access behind a sales call adds real time to your evaluation before you've even seen how the API behaves. Ask for a realistic integration timeline based on other clients of similar size and stack, not a best-case number.

  • What does this actually cost at the volume you run, not the headline per-call rate? A low advertised per-call price can look very different once minimum commitments, tiered discounts, add-on product costs, and support-tier fees are factored in at your real monthly verification volume. Ask for a worked estimate at your actual numbers, not a rate card.

Any vendor confident in what they've built should be able to answer these directly. Vague or deflected answers to any of the five are worth treating as a data point in themselves.

Conclusion

There isn't a universally "right" answer to VerifyEdge versus HyperVerge versus Karza (Perfios) versus Signzy - the right choice depends on what your institution is actually optimizing for. If biometric liveness and deepfake defense is your single biggest concern, HyperVerge's specialization there is real and worth a serious look. If deep financial-document analysis at enterprise scale matters most, Perfios brings genuine depth there. If broad compliance screening in one platform is the priority, Signzy's positioning is built for exactly that.

If what you value most is a single platform covering identity, business (KYB), and bank verification, built with transparent, published pricing so your procurement process can move without waiting on a sales call for a rough estimate - that's where Verify Edge fits well, and it's a genuinely honest fit for many mid-size and growing lenders rather than a claim that it beats every competitor on every axis. Frequently Asked Questions

Should a bank choose one all-in-one verification API or multiple specialized point solutions?

It depends on what you're solving for. A single platform covering KYC, business verification, and bank account checks reduces integration overhead and vendor management - one contract, one API, one support line. A specialized point solution can make sense if you have one narrow, high-stakes problem (deepfake detection at account opening, say) that a broader platform doesn't cover as deeply. Many banks land on a hybrid - a primary platform for day-to-day verification, with a specialist bolted on for one specific risk. Neither approach is wrong; the mistake is picking one without mapping it against your actual fraud and onboarding volumes first.

Why does pricing transparency matter when evaluating a KYC API vendor?

Because budget approval inside a bank or NBFC usually needs a number before anyone will schedule a vendor call, not after. Published, indicative per-call pricing lets a product or risk team build a rough cost model against expected verification volumes in an afternoon, then bring that estimate into an internal sign-off conversation. Custom-quote pricing isn't necessarily worse - it's common in enterprise software for good reasons - but it does add a sales cycle to what could otherwise be a quick comparison, which matters when you're screening five vendors and not just evaluating your one preferred choice.

Is a larger, more established vendor always the safer choice for a bank?

Not automatically, though it's an understandable instinct - scale can mean more engineering resources behind the product and more institutions already relying on it. But scale doesn't by itself guarantee better SLAs, faster support response, or pricing that fits your specific volume. A smaller, more focused vendor can offer more responsive support and pricing built for your segment precisely because you're not one account among thousands of enterprise clients. The honest answer is to evaluate the actual commitments in front of you - SLA terms, support model, integration support - rather than defaulting to vendor size as a proxy for reliability.

What should a bank ask about SLA and uptime before signing with a verification API provider?

Ask for the request-level SLA in writing, not just a marketing claim of uptime - what response time is guaranteed for a single verification call, what happens during a source-registry outage (say, the GST portal or a bank's penny-drop system is down) that's outside the vendor's own control, and what the actual remedy is if the SLA is breached, whether that's a credit, an escalation path, or nothing at all. Also ask how uptime is measured and reported - self-reported dashboards and independently monitored status pages are not the same thing, and it's worth asking which one you're getting.

How long does it typically take to integrate a new verification API vendor?

For a single well-documented REST API with sandbox access, a few days to a couple of weeks of engineering time is a reasonable expectation for a basic integration - identity or business verification calls wired into an onboarding flow. It stretches out when a vendor's sandbox access itself requires a sales conversation before you can even see the API responses, when documentation is thin, or when your own compliance and security review adds its own timeline on top of the technical work. Ask upfront whether sandbox access is instant and self-serve or gated behind a call - that answer alone tells you a lot about how the rest of the integration will go.
